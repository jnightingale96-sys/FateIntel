### Japan, China, South Korea, India — scoping only

**Japan.** The Soil Contamination Countermeasures Act (Act No. 53 of 2002, Ministry of the Environment) is Japan's national contaminated-land statute. It defines "Designated Hazardous Substances" (currently ~26 substances across volatile organic compounds, heavy metals, and pesticides) and **explicitly excludes radioactive substances** from that definition (Article 2(1): "...excluding radioactive substances..."). It has a three-tier structure: (1) mandatory soil contamination investigation triggers (defunct specified-facility sites, land-form changes, health-risk situations — Articles 3–5); (2) area designation — "Areas which Require Action" where contamination exceeds standards and poses a health threat (Article 6) vs. "Areas for which Changes to Form or Nature Require Notification" where standards are exceeded but no current health threat exists (Article 11); (3) mandatory remediation/countermeasures and regulated transport/processing of contaminated soil (Articles 7, 16–28). Native-buildable assessment: **external-handoff-only** — no numeric thresholds were pulled into this matrix, would need Ministry of the Environment ordinance-level standards. Source: https://www.japaneselawtranslation.go.jp/en/laws/view/2038/en (primary translated statute, read directly).

**China.** GB 36600-2018, "Soil environmental quality — Risk control standard for soil contamination of development land (Trial)," issued by the Ministry of Ecology and Environment (MEE) with the State Administration for Market Regulation (SAMR)/Standardization Administration of China (SAC), approved 17 May 2018, effective 1 August 2018. It establishes risk screening values and intervention values across two land-use categories (Category I: residential/schools/medical/parks; Category II: industrial/commercial/warehousing), covering heavy metals/inorganics (As, Cd, Cr, Cu, Pb, Hg, Ni), volatile/semi-volatile organics, PAHs, pesticides, and PCBs, plus petroleum hydrocarbons per secondary summaries. A companion standard, GB 15618-2018, covers agricultural land specifically. Exceeding a screening value triggers detailed investigation/risk evaluation; exceeding an intervention value triggers mandatory remediation. Native-buildable: **external-handoff-only**; the standard's full numeric tables were not fetched (paywalled on the commercial standard-resale sites; official free full text is on openstd.samr.gov.cn but was not opened this session). Sources: https://www.codeofchina.com/standard/GB36600-2018.html ; official portal located at https://openstd.samr.gov.cn/bzgk/gb/newGbInfo?hcno=EAEFE2655651E09C7798C158BF8369B6 (link located, not opened/read this session).

**South Korea.** The Soil Environment Conservation Act (current consolidated version read directly via the Korea Legislation Research Institute's official English translation portal, elaw.klri.re.kr) establishes a national two-tier soil contamination standard: a "Worrisome Level" (Article 4-2 — soil contamination "likely to damage the health and property of persons or to hinder the growth of animals and plants") and a stricter "Levels Requiring Measures" (Article 16 — contamination exceeding the worrisome level, triggering mandatory remediation/area designation). Both thresholds are set by ministerial decree (not enumerated in the Act text itself) and administered by the Ministry of Environment. Secondary sources (Lexology commentary, not independently re-verified against the decree text) describe 17 designated soil pollutants (including cadmium, copper, arsenic, mercury, oil, organic solvents) and land-use-differentiated standards (farm/woodland "Ga" areas vs. factory/road/rail "Na" areas). Native-buildable: **external-handoff-only**. Source: https://elaw.klri.re.kr/eng_service/lawTwoView.do?hseq=14745 (primary, read directly).

**India.** No comparably binding national contaminated-land statute was confirmed this session. The Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) — operating under the general Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, and coordinating State Pollution Control Boards — has issued non-binding technical guidance on identification, inspection, and assessment of contaminated sites, referencing site-specific target levels (SSTLs) derived via either a risk-based or standard-based approach, and maintains a Comprehensive Environmental Pollution Index (CEPI) covering industrial-area land quality alongside air/water. I fetched the actual CPCB guidelines PDF but the extracted text was garbled/unreliable for direct quotation, so the document's own title/date/precise scope should be treated as **unconfirmed pending a cleaner PDF re-extraction**. Native-buildable: not applicable — no confirmed national numeric framework to build against; would be state-by-state guidance at best. Sources: https://cpcb.nic.in/uploads/hwmd/CPCB_guidelines_contaminatedsites.pdf (redirects to https://cpcb.gov.in/uploads/hwmd/CPCB_guidelines_contaminatedsites.pdf — fetched, text quality poor) ; https://cpcb.nic.in/uploads/hwmd/Brief_Contmainated_sites_in_india.pdf (located, not opened this session).

## PART B — Radionuclides as a distinct specialist pathway

**The founder's claim, restated**: when radionuclide/radioactive contamination is detected, route it to a specialist radiological assessment pathway rather than treating it like an ordinary chemical. This session's research finds the claim **holds structurally in Canada and the UK, holds at the IAEA policy-guidance level, but is meaningfully qualified by the US EPA's actual Superfund practice** — which is important, since FateIntel's own strongest jurisdiction (in terms of existing coverage depth) orientation should not assume the Canada/UK pattern is universal.

**Canada.** Health Canada, under FCSAP, publishes radiological risk assessment guidance as a **separate document in its own right**: "Federal Contaminated Site Risk Assessment in Canada, Part VI: Guidance on Human Health Detailed Quantitative Radiological Risk Assessment (DQRARAD)" (2010, Cat. No. H128-1/11-634E-PDF), distinct from Part I (PQRA, chemical) and the chemical DQRA guidance. Health Canada's own page description states the document "provides a background to the principles applied to radiological risk assessments and **a comparison with conventional chemical risk assessments**" — confirming the two are treated as parallel-but-distinct methodologies within the same FCSAP program, though the page text itself does not use the words "must be separated" verbatim; the separateness is evidenced by the document being its own numbered Part in the series rather than a section within the chemical guidance. Source: https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/environmental-workplace-health/reports-publications/contaminated-sites/federal-contaminated-site-risk-assessment-canada-part-guidance-human-health-detailed-quantitative-radiological-risk-assessment-dqrarad-health-canada.html (read directly).

**United Kingdom.** Confirmed directly via gov.uk: radioactive contaminated land is regulated under Part 2A of the Environmental Protection Act 1990, as extended by the Radioactive Contaminated Land (Enabling Powers) (England) Regulations 2005 and the Radioactive Contaminated Land (Modification of Enactments) (England) Regulations 2006, with its own statutory guidance (published by the then-Department for Business, Energy and Industrial Strategy). The gov.uk guidance page states directly: "More stringent standards of remediation than those under Part 2A apply to the management of the risks posed by man-made radioactive substances as a result of redevelopment for a new use." This is explicit primary-source confirmation that radioactive contamination is handled as a **distinct, more stringent sub-regime**, not folded into ordinary chemical Part 2A assessment. Institutionally, nuclear-licensed sites are regulated by the Office for Nuclear Regulation (ONR) rather than the Environment Agency/SEPA/Natural Resources Wales — this institutional split is corroborated by search-result snippets (ONR's "Land quality management" page, an EA CLR-14 methodology report reference) but was not independently opened and read this session. Source (primary, read directly): https://www.gov.uk/guidance/land-affected-by-contamination.

**United States.** This is the key nuance. EPA's own CLU-IN training resource, "Correcting Some Misconceptions about EPA's Superfund Approach for Radiation Risk Assessment," explicitly addresses and pushes back on the assumption that radiological contamination is handled in a segregated pathway. Per the page (read directly): EPA treats radionuclides at CERCLA/Superfund sites using the **same** risk-assessment framework as chemical carcinogens — the same 10⁻⁴ to 10⁻⁶ risk range, expressed in the same risk-level units rather than millirem/millisievert (which are used under EPA's other, non-Superfund statutory authorities) — with the key *technical* (not procedural) difference being that radionuclide risk uses slope factors derived from Federal Guidance Report 13 in place of chemical toxicity values. The page explicitly frames this as correcting a **misconception** commonly held even by radiation professionals, "citing the wrong EPA documents or portions of documents incorrectly." **Conclusion for FateIntel's design**: the "always route radionuclides to a fully separate specialist pathway" framing is accurate for Canada and the UK's institutional/regulatory structure, but the US EPA case shows a jurisdiction where radionuclides are integrated into the *same* overarching risk-assessment framework with substituted (not separately-pathed) toxicity/dose tools. A one-size-fits-all "always specialist pathway" design assumption would misrepresent US Superfund practice. Source (primary, read directly): https://www.clu-in.org/conf/tio/RadRA/.

**European Union.** Council Directive 2013/59/Euratom ("Basic Safety Standards Directive") is the EU's consolidated radiation protection framework, replacing five earlier Euratom directives, and covers occupational, medical, public, and environmental exposure to ionising radiation, including discharge authorisation and monitoring for radioactive effluents. It operates under the Euratom Treaty, a separate treaty base from the TFEU-derived chemical/environmental regime (e.g., REACH). This structural separateness (different treaty base, different competent authorities in most member states — nuclear/radiation regulators vs. environment/chemicals regulators) is a well-established feature of EU law, but **the directive's own text was not successfully fetched this session** (two EUR-Lex fetch attempts returned empty content) — so the specific scope-article language could not be quoted directly. Flagged as needing follow-up. Source (located, not read): https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2013/59/oj.

**IAEA (international posture).** The IAEA has published a structured suite of Safety Guides specifically for radiological (not chemical) environmental impact assessment: GSG-8 "Radiation Protection of the Public and the Environment," GSG-9 "Regulatory Control of Radioactive Discharges to the Environment," and GSG-10 "Prospective Radiological Environmental Impact Assessment for Facilities and Activities." Their existence, numbering, and one-line scope descriptions were confirmed via IAEA's own search/newscenter results and publication catalog pages (iaea.org/publications/12197 for GSG-9, iaea.org/publications/12198 for GSG-10). GSG-10 in particular "details the general framework and methodologies for prospective radiological impact assessment... to estimate and control the radiological effects on the public and on the environment" — a purpose-built framework distinct from IAEA's (separate) chemical hazard guidance. A direct fetch of the GSG-8 news article returned an HTTP 402 (payment required) error, so full text was not read; titles/scope are confirmed via search snippets and publication catalog metadata only. Sources (catalog pages located, not fully read): https://www.iaea.org/publications/12197/regulatory-control-of-radioactive-discharges-to-the-environment ; https://www.iaea.org/publications/12198/prospective-radiological-environmental-impact-assessment-for-facilities-and-activities.

**Overall synthesis for Part B.** The international posture is genuinely mixed, not uniform:
- **Structural/institutional separation (supports founder's framing as-is)**: Canada (separate FCSAP Part VI document), UK (separate, more stringent Part 2A sub-regime + separate regulator for nuclear-licensed sites), IAEA (dedicated GSG series).
- **Same framework, substituted tools (qualifies founder's framing)**: US EPA/CERCLA — radionuclides are assessed within the *same* Superfund risk-assessment architecture as chemicals, just with radionuclide-specific slope factors instead of chemical toxicity values, and EPA explicitly warns against assuming otherwise.
- **Unconfirmed**: EU Euratom's precise structural relationship to chemical environmental law (very likely separate, given separate treaty base, but not confirmed by reading the directive text itself this session).

For FateIntel's product design, the safest accurate framing is: **"when radionuclide contamination is detected, do not silently run it through chemical PNEC/HHRA math — flag it for a distinct radiological calculation path (different inputs: activity concentrations/dose coefficients, not concentration/toxicity-reference-values), because every jurisdiction researched uses radionuclide-specific quantitative tools even where (as in the US) the overarching risk-range framework is nominally shared with chemicals."** This is a defensible middle-ground statement supported directly by every jurisdiction examined, including the US counterexample.
